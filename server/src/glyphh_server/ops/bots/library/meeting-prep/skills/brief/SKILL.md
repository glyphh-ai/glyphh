# The meeting brief

A brief gets the person ready for one meeting in the minute before it starts: who is coming, what it is for, and
what to get out of it. All of the day's briefs go on one page.

## The page

- **Title**: `Meeting briefs, <the date>` (for example `Meeting briefs, 2026-11-23`).
- **Content**: one brief per meeting, in the order the meetings happen.

## One brief

```
## 14:00 Contract review with Acme

**Who**: Dana Kim (Acme), Sam Ortiz, you.
**Why**: agree the renewal terms before Friday's deadline.
**Ask**:
- Which of the three open clauses is Acme's blocker?
- Who signs on their side?
- What happens if we miss Friday?
```

- **Heading**: the start time and the event's title.
- **Who**: the attendees, on one line. Names when the event gives them, else the addresses.
- **Why**: what the meeting is for, from the event's description, in one line.
- **Ask**: three short questions that would make the meeting worth having.

## When the event says little

Use only what the event says. When it has no description, write `**Why**: the invite does not say.` and make the
three questions the ones that would find out (what is the goal, what is decided here, what is needed from me).

## Length and tone

- A brief is under 80 words. Prepared, brief, practical: plain words, no filler.
- No introduction to the page and no closing line.

## What to leave out

- Meetings decided as not needing a brief: never list them.
- Anything about the attendees or the company that the event does not hold. Never guess a role or a history.
- Dial-in links and room details: the calendar has them.
